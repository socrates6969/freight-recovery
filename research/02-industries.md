# 02 Buyer segments

Status: pre-product. The product, scoup.ai, uses synthetic data only, never deployed, no paying customers. The revenue ceilings below are arithmetic on stated inputs. Every input is labelled SOURCED (with a source line) or ASSUMED (our number, untested). No ceiling here has been validated by a customer.

Research date: 2026-10-17.

Strategy (owner decision, 2026-10-17): shipper- and importer-first. Mid-market shippers and SME ocean importers are the core segments; small and mid-size carriers become a later segment, approached through partners. The sections below follow that order.

Reconciliation note (updated 2026-10-17): the investor-package TAM, SAM and SOM are in 05-market-size.md section 4 and use the finance model’s revenue per account. The account counts for shippers (19,932, new in this version), importers, carriers and brokers come from this file. The importer, carrier and broker ceilings below still use our own bottom-up fees (20% contingency, $12,000 broker fee) and are a cross-check, not package numbers; the shipper ceiling uses the finance model’s revenue per account.

We size four segments: mid-market shippers, SME ocean importers, small and mid-size carriers, and freight brokers. The shipper count is a model built from Census revenue classes and an old freight-to-sales ratio; the importer and carrier counts come straight from Census and FMCSA data.

### Mid-market shippers ($5M to $50M annual freight spend) — core segment
- What the industry does, in 3 sentences: These are manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers that buy truckload, LTL and ocean capacity from carriers, brokers and forwarders, but are too small for the enterprise freight audit programs of the incumbents. Their accounts-payable team receives carrier invoices that carry accessorials (detention, lumper, liftgate, reweigh, redelivery, fuel surcharge) on top of the agreed rate. They need to separate owed charges from unsupported ones and short-pay or dispute with proof.
- Industry size (figure, source, date): $189.9B of estimated annual freight spend across the firms in the band (our arithmetic, below), against $883.2B of total US trucking spend in 2025 (CSCMP; see shared inputs). The firms in the band had $4,057.4B of receipts in 2022: manufacturing $1,268.9B, wholesale $1,848.3B, retail $940.2B (Census SUSB 2022, enterprise receipts classes $100M to $999.9M). Freight spend = $4,057.4B x 4.68% = $189.9B, about 21% of US trucking spend. The 4.68% ratio is ASSUMED to apply; see the next bullet for its source.
  Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Statistics of U.S. Businesses 2022, "Number of Firms and Establishments, Employment, Annual Payroll, and Receipts by Industry and Enterprise Receipts Size", release 10 April 2025, https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/tables/2022/us_naicssector_large_rcptsize_2022.xlsx, accessed 2026-10-17 (copy in research/data/, extraction in research/scripts/susb_shipper_counts.py)
- Number of addressable companies (figure, source, date): 19,932 firms (manufacturing 5,971, wholesale 8,798, retail 5,163). No public dataset counts shippers by freight spend, so we convert the freight-spend band into a revenue band and count firms by revenue.
  Step 1, freight spend as a share of sales: Establish Inc.’s logistics cost survey, presented at CSCMP, put transportation at 4.68% of sales for the average participating company (manufacturers, distributors and retailers): "transportation is 4.68 percent of sales, warehousing is 1.9 percent, and inventory is 1.8 percent" (DC Velocity, 2008). This is the newest version of that ratio we could read at the source; a 2014 figure of 4.43% appears only in a slide deck we could not open. Grade: SOURCED-B, but 18 years old, and we ASSUME it still holds.
  Step 2, revenue band: $5M / 4.68% = $106.8M and $50M / 4.68% = $1.068B, which we round to the Census receipts classes $100M to $999.9M.
  Step 3, firm count: Census SUSB 2022 firms in those classes, NAICS 31-33, 42 and 44-45 = 19,932.
  Sensitivity: at 2% freight-to-sales the band becomes $250M to $2.5B and the count 9,366; at 10% it becomes $50M to $500M and the count 35,349 (the 10% case uses the $50M to $99.9M classes from the 6-digit SUSB receipts file, https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/susb/tables/2022/us_6digitnaics_rcptsize_2022.xlsx). Establish also reports that logistics costs run higher as a share of sales for smaller companies, which argues for the higher counts; retailers’ lower transport share (a food-retail benchmark of 1.5% from 2001, too old to rely on) argues for fewer retail firms.
  Source: DC Velocity, "Logistics costs and services 2008" (Establish Inc. study presented at CSCMP), https://www.dcvelocity.com/articles/31001-logistics-costs-and-services-2008, accessed 2026-10-17
  Tried and rejected: the BEA 2023 summary use table. In its purchasers’-price framework, freight on purchased goods sits inside the goods rows, so truck transportation shows as 0.03% of manufacturing output, which measures the accounting convention, not shippers’ freight bills. Files kept outside the repo; noted in open-questions.md.
  Source: U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, Supply-Use Tables 1997-2023 (AllTablesSUP.zip), https://apps.bea.gov/industry/iTables%20Static%20Files/AllTablesSUP.zip, accessed 2026-10-17
- Our realistic revenue ceiling here (show the arithmetic): average freight spend per firm = $189.9B / 19,932 = $9.53M. At 1% recoverable unsupported accessorials and errors (ASSUMED; audit vendors claim 1% to 9%, grade C) a typical account recovers $95.3K a year, which sits inside the finance model’s recovery path of $85K (year 3) to $115K (year 7). Using the finance model’s year-7 revenue per shipper account of $27.6K: 19,932 x $27.6K = $550.1M a year at 100% penetration. The finance base case has 304 shipper accounts in year 7 (financial/scenarios.md), 1.5% of these firms; the model prices a shipper at $27.6K in year 7, below the importer’s $30.8K, because a shipper has no ocean D&D to recover.
  Source: finance worktree, financial/assumptions.md (inputs recov, share, fee) and financial/scenarios.md, dated 2026-10-17
- Competitors active in this industry specifically: Loop (now carries Data2Logistics, which served small, mid-size and enterprise customers), Intelligent Audit (Catalyst for SMBs, parcel only so far), CTSI-Global and nVision Global (services-led), Cass (enterprise-weighted). Mid-market fit for each is in 01-competitors.md.
- Why this industry buys software like ours: RXO’s November 2024 survey of 750 shippers and carriers found that "61% of shippers agree that managing accessorial requests is one of the biggest time-wasters for their accounting team" and that 84% of shippers see a moderate (65%) or significant (19%) budget impact from accessorials; the same survey found 70% of shippers think carriers are usually honest, so the pain is workload more than fraud (details and source in 04-gaps.md, Gap 5). Vendors also describe accessorial verification as manual: Banyan Technology, a TMS vendor, frames the goal as turning "accessorial verification from a manual, reactive task into a proactive and automated process" (April 2025). That one is a vendor framing, not buyer testimony.
  Source: Banyan Technology blog, "Why accessorial verification is critical to controlling freight spend", 22 April 2025, https://blog.banyantechnology.com/why-accessorial-verification-is-critical-to-controlling-freight-spend, accessed 2026-10-17

### Ocean importers paying container demurrage and detention — core segment
- What the industry does, in 3 sentences: These are US companies that import goods in ocean containers, mostly manufacturers and wholesalers buying from overseas suppliers. When a container sits at the terminal past its free days (demurrage) or the empty box is returned late (detention), the ocean carrier or terminal invoices a per-day fee. Since May 2024 the FMC billing rule fixes what a valid invoice must contain and sets 30-day windows to issue, dispute and answer it.
- Industry size (figure, source, date): the FMC reports that the nine largest ocean carriers "have collected roughly $15.4 billion in D&D charges between April 1, 2020, and March 31, 2025". Over those five years that averages $3.08B a year (our arithmetic: $15.4B / 5). The FMC page does not say whether the figure covers US trades only; we assume it does because the FMC collects it under its US jurisdiction, and we list that as an open question.
  Source: Federal Maritime Commission, Detention and Demurrage, https://www.fmc.gov/detention-and-demurrage/, accessed 2026-10-17
- Number of addressable companies (figure, source, date): 240,535 identified US importing companies in 2024, of which 234,023 are small and medium enterprises (under 500 employees) and 6,512 are large. SME importers accounted for $884B of the $2,926B known import value (30.2%, our arithmetic). Manufacturers made up 17.4% of importers and wholesalers 36.5%. The Census count is all modes; the number that import by ocean container is UNKNOWN — needs primary research.
  Source: U.S. Census Bureau, "A Profile of U.S. Importing and Exporting Companies, 2023-2024", release CB26-73, 5 May 2026, https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/edbrel2024.pdf, accessed 2026-10-17
- Importers by size, for the finance model (added 2026-10-17): the business case’s importer moves about 2,000 TEU a year, and finance asked how many US importers bring in 500 to 5,000 TEU. No public source counts importers by container volume or by import value; Census publishes counts by employment size only. What can be built from public data:
  Value per TEU: US containerized vessel imports were worth $975.3B in 2023 (BTS, sum of East $480.9B, Gulf $105.0B, West $387.4B and Great Lakes $2.0B). At 28.9 million TEU imported in 2023 (S&P Global Market Intelligence, via a search summary we could not open at the source) that is $33.7K per TEU (our arithmetic). So 500 to 5,000 TEU is about $16.9M to $168.7M of containerized imports a year, and 2,000 TEU is about $67.5M. A different tracker (Descartes) reports 28.2 million TEU for 2024, so the per-TEU value is approximate.
  Importers by employment size, 2024 (Census Table 1d), with average import value per importer (our arithmetic): 100 to 249 employees, 9,607 importers, average $14.8M; 250 to 499 employees, 4,160 importers, average $33.1M; 500 or more employees, 6,512 importers, average $313.6M. Averages are of all import modes, not only containers.
  Bound: the 500 to 5,000 TEU band sits above the average SME importer and below the average large importer. A loose range is 4,160 (only the 250 to 499 class) to 20,279 (all importers with 100 or more employees). The FMC-pool cap used in 05-market-size.md, 12,833 importers at the business case’s $240K D&D exposure, falls inside that range, which is why we keep it as the SAM importer count. It also means the business-case importer is closer to a "large" importer by Census’s employment definition than to a typical SME. An exact count needs bill-of-lading data (for example PIERS or ImportGenius) and is listed in open-questions.md.
  Source: Bureau of Transportation Statistics, "Value of Containerized Imports by Coast in 2023" (dataset ub6a-sqr5), https://data.transportation.gov/resource/ub6a-sqr5.json, accessed 2026-10-17
  Source: U.S. Census Bureau, "A Profile of U.S. Importing and Exporting Companies, 2023-2024", Table 1d (edbrelxlsx2024.zip), https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/Press-Release/edb/edbrelxlsx2024.zip, accessed 2026-10-17
  Source: search summary of S&P Global Market Intelligence data, "For all of calendar year 2023, imports fell 8.5%, to 28.9 million TEU", reported via Logistics Management, https://www.logisticsmgmt.com/topic/tag/Twenty-Foot_Equivalent_Units/P45, accessed 2026-10-17 (primary not opened)
- Our realistic revenue ceiling here (show the arithmetic):
  $3.08B a year collected (SOURCED, FMC) x 10% recoverable through a well-founded dispute (ASSUMED; a 30% to 40% "inaccurate invoice" estimate circulates in FMC docket commentary, but we could not open the primary comment, so we do not use it) x 20% fee (ASSUMED) = $61.6M a year at 100% share of the whole market.
  Restricting to SME importers by their share of import value: $61.6M x 30.2% = $18.6M a year at 100% share of SMEs.
  Realistic ceiling: 5% of the SME slice by year 5 (ASSUMED) = $0.93M a year. On these inputs ocean D&D is a feature that deepens an importer account, not a standalone business. BlueCargo’s claim of "$350M+ in Detention & Demurrage Savings" (vendor claim, grade C) hints that our 10% recoverable input may be low, which makes it the input to test first.
- Competitors active in this industry specifically: BlueCargo, Portcast (adjacent), Intelligent Audit (D&D invoice validation case study), nVision Global (D&D audit content).
- Why this industry buys software like ours: the FMC rule makes compliance checkable by rule. In Thompson Hine’s words, "Failure of the billing party to abide by the invoice content and timing requirements relieves the billed party’s obligation to pay an invoice for detention and demurrage charges", and the billed party has 30 calendar days from issuance to request mitigation, refund or waiver. A September 2025 D.C. Circuit decision vacated only the section on who may be billed (46 C.F.R. 541.4); the content and dispute provisions remain in force. Deadline tracking and field-by-field invoice checks are what our D&D module and claims state machine already do on synthetic data.
  Source: Thompson Hine, "FMC Issues Final Rule on Billing Requirements and Practices for Detention and Demurrage Invoices", https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/fmc-issues-final-rule-on-billing-requirements-and-practices-for-detention-and-demurrage-invoices/, accessed 2026-10-17
  Source: Thompson Hine, "Court Sets Aside Part of FMC’s Demurrage and Detention Billing Rule", 26 September 2025, https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/court-sets-aside-part-of-fmcs-demurrage-and-detention-billing-rule/, accessed 2026-10-17

### Small and mid-size for-hire truckload carriers (5 to 100 power units) — later segment, partner channel
- What the industry does, in 3 sentences: These are trucking companies that haul freight for shippers and brokers under their own operating authority, usually with 5 to 100 tractors. They bill a linehaul rate plus accessorials such as detention, layover, lumper reimbursement and truck-ordered-not-used, and they wait on payment from brokers or shippers. Most run a small back office, often the owner plus a dispatcher and a biller, with telematics from an ELD vendor.
- Industry size (figure, source, date): US trucking spend of $883.2B in 2025 (CSCMP, see shared inputs; includes private fleets, so it overstates the for-hire market). ATRI puts unreimbursed direct detention expense for the industry at $3.6B in 2023.
  Source: as in shared inputs, accessed 2026-10-17
- Number of addressable companies (figure, source, date): 72,053 active interstate for-hire carriers with 5 to 100 power units that filed an MCS-150 update since 17 October 2024; together they report 1,206,250 power units (average 16.7). Without the recency filter the count is 80,296. Of the 72,053, 57,712 list general freight as a cargo type. The full active interstate for-hire population is 532,506, of which 403,115 have 1 to 4 power units and 3,752 have more than 100. We queried the FMCSA file directly on 2026-10-17; the latest MCS-150 date in the file was 15 October 2026. The query is saved in research/scripts/fmcsa_carrier_counts.sh.
  Source: FMCSA, Company Census File (dataset az4n-8mr2), https://data.transportation.gov/resource/az4n-8mr2.json, accessed 2026-10-17
  Cross-check: ATA's American Trucking Trends 2025, as reported by FleetOwner, says 91.5% of carriers operate 10 or fewer trucks; FleetOwner also cites FMCSA at about 1.1 million for-hire carriers, a count that includes intrastate and inactive-looking registrations.
  Source: FleetOwner, "Trucking By the Numbers 2025 Overview", https://fleetowner.com/research/truck-by-numbers/media-gallery/55338098/trucking-by-the-numbers-2025-overview-carrier-composition-costs-and-impact, accessed 2026-10-17
- Our realistic revenue ceiling here (show the arithmetic):
  Inputs per power unit per year: 400 stops (ASSUMED: about 2 stops a working day over 200 days) x 39.3% detained (SOURCED, ATRI) x $100 billable detention per detained stop (ASSUMED; ATRI's 2019 survey found an average detention fee of $63.71 an hour, so $100 is about 1.6 billable hours) x 50% unpaid (SOURCED as a conservative floor: ATRI says fewer than 50 percent of detention invoices are paid, so the unpaid share is above 50%; billed-but-unpaid and never-billed are not separated in the release) x 30% winnable with an evidence packet (ASSUMED) = $2,358 recoverable per power unit per year.
  Fee: $2,358 x 20% (ASSUMED) = $471.60 per power unit per year, or $7,895 per average carrier (16.74 power units).
  Whole segment at 100% penetration: 1,206,250 power units x $471.60 = $568.9M a year. This is a theoretical maximum, not a target.
  Realistic ceiling: 3% of the 72,053 carriers by year 5 (ASSUMED) = 2,162 carriers x $7,895 = $17.1M a year in fees.
  Source for the $63.71 figure: J. J. Keller Compliance Network, "ATRI: Driver detention frequency, length on the rise", 5 September 2019, https://jjkellercompliancenetwork.com/news/atri-driver-detention-frequency-length-on-the-rise, accessed 2026-10-17
- Competitors active in this industry specifically: Hey Bubba (geofence detention claims inside an AI dispatcher), FleetOptos (Geotab-based detention billing), Detention Source (iOS app). None of the six FAP firms in 01-competitors.md sells to carriers.
- Why this industry buys software like ours: the money is plainly owed to them and they lose it for want of proof. In DAT's 2016 survey of 257 carriers, 84% named detention a top-five business problem and "Only three percent of carriers were paid on 90 percent or more of their detention claims". In a 2025 TruckersReport forum poll, the most-chosen reason for denied claims (7 of 13 votes, 53.8%) was "They just ignore my request / It's too much hassle to fight for it". The business plan assumes carriers have small software budgets, which is why it prices this segment on contingency; that assumption is untested.
  Source: DAT Solutions, "63 Percent of Drivers are Detained for More Than 3 Hours Per Stop", 14 July 2016, https://www.dat.com/company/news-events/news-releases/63-percent-of-drivers-are-detained-for-more-than-3-hours-per-stop-dat-solutions, accessed 2026-10-17
  Source: TheTruckersReport forum, "What's the #1 reason your detention claims get denied? (poll)", https://www.thetruckersreport.com/truckingindustryforum/threads/whats-the-1-reason-your-detention-claims-get-denied-poll.2529156/page-3, accessed 2026-10-17

### Freight brokers and 3PLs
- What the industry does, in 3 sentences: Brokers arrange truckload capacity for shippers, paying carriers and billing shippers for the same load. On detention they sit in the middle, approving or rejecting the carrier’s claim and trying to rebill the shipper. Many run thin margins and an operations team that handles accessorial approvals by email.
- Industry size (figure, source, date): UNKNOWN — needs primary research (we did not reach a primary source for brokerage gross revenue this session).
- Number of addressable companies (figure, source, date): 28,438 active freight brokerage authorities in January 2024, down 7.8% year on year (Carrier Details and Brush Pass Research). This is the newest figure we found; the FMCSA census dataset we queried for carriers returned only 3 active rows classed as brokers, so it is not the right file for broker counts.
  Source: FreightCaviar, "Brush Pass Research: 7.8% fewer brokerages than a year ago", 24 January 2024, https://freightcaviar.com/brush-pass-research-7-8-fewer-brokerages-than-a-year-ago, accessed 2026-10-17
- Our realistic revenue ceiling here (show the arithmetic): brokers are a software sale, not a contingency sale, because the recovered money flows through them. At $12,000 a year per broker (ASSUMED, from the plan’s phase-2 platform fee of $1,000 a month), 28,438 brokers x $12,000 = $341.3M at 100% penetration. Realistic ceiling at 1% by year 5 (ASSUMED) = 284 brokers and $3.4M a year.
- Competitors active in this industry specifically: Loop (sells to brokers, per Sacra) and BlueCargo (drayage brokers). TMS vendors are the default substitute; we did not research them this session.
- Why this industry buys software like ours: DAT found that "Two-thirds of brokers surveyed said they paid detention only when they could collect the fee from the shipper or consignee". A broker who can rebill the shipper with a cited packet keeps the carrier relationship without absorbing the cost. The same survey found only 20% of 50 responding brokers ranked detention a top-five problem, against 84% of carriers, which warns that brokers may feel the pain less than the carriers they pay.
  Source: DAT Solutions, 14 July 2016, https://www.dat.com/company/news-events/news-releases/63-percent-of-drivers-are-detained-for-more-than-3-hours-per-stop-dat-solutions, accessed 2026-10-17

## Reading the four together

The two core segments now have counts: 19,932 mid-market shippers (a modelled count, sensitive to the freight-to-sales ratio: 9,366 at 2%, 35,349 at 10%) and 234,023 SME importers, of which the FMC D&D pool can support only about 12,833 at the business case’s exposure (05-market-size.md section 5). The shipper count rests on a ratio from 2008, so it is the input to refresh first, ideally with a purchased firmographic list filtered by revenue and industry. Shippers carry the largest dollars per account: an average of $9.53M of freight spend, so about $95K recovered at an assumed 1%, which sits inside the finance model’s recovery path.

Carriers keep the best demand evidence and the best data, but the smallest dollars per account, and the owner has moved them to a later, partner-led segment. Brokers sit between carrier and shipper and stay outside the SAM until a pilot shows they buy.

The first job of a design-partner pilot is to replace the ASSUMED inputs that drive these figures: the recoverable share of shipper spend, the recoverable share of D&D charges, the fee path, and the freight-to-sales ratio behind the shipper count.
