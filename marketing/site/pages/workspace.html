Claims and documents

Saved claims with revisions and timelines

Prototype, early access

Each check becomes a claim with a number, an assignee and a status. Every edit creates a new packet revision and keeps the previous one as superseded, so the timeline shows who changed what, when, and why. A revision moves from pending review to approved or rejected; an approved one can be marked send-ready, and an edit at any point starts a new revision.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
The same figures as the browser tool, or documents accepted from the import queue; a reason with every revision.
You get
A claim list to filter, sort and assign; the revision history of each claim; a content-hashed evidence packet per revision.
It does not
Send anything. Marking a demand send-ready is the last step; the API imports no mailer or HTTP client, and a test asserts that.

Document import

Prototype, early access

PDF text, CSV and plain-text files are parsed in a separate worker process with time, memory and size limits and no network access. The file type comes from its bytes, never from the name the client sends. Every extracted field goes to a review queue; nothing reaches a claim until a person has confirmed or corrected it. Accepted documents are frozen by a database trigger and grouped by load number into claims.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
Files in batches, the document type when the detection is wrong, and a correction with its reason for any field.
You get
A review queue, the accepted documents linked to each claim, and an append-only record of every review decision that stores only hashes of the values before and after.
It does not
Accept a value on its own, read scanned images (no OCR), take attachments from a mailbox, or connect to a TMS.

Ocean D&D audit inside a claim

Prototype, early access

The same eight checks as the browser tool, run on a demurrage or detention invoice entered in a claim: the content 46 CFR 541.6 requires, the 30-day issuance rule in 541.7, invoice and line arithmetic, and a recomputation against a public carrier tariff. The person who entered the invoice is recorded and cannot approve the claim.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
The invoice fields, each container's charged periods, your own clock evidence and, optionally, a tariff preset.
You get
Findings with the clause quoted and the cited values, inside the claim's packet and timeline.
It does not
Decide whether a charge is legally owed. Confirm current rules and any dispute with counsel or your customs broker.

Source: Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 46 CFR Part 541, current through 2026-10-15, https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-46/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-541.

Four-eyes approval

Prototype, early access

A revision is approved by someone other than the people who shaped it. The author of the current revision, the person who accepted its source documents, the person who entered its ocean D&D invoice and anyone whose charge label influenced its findings cannot approve it. The rule is checked in the application and again by database triggers, so a request that skips the application still fails.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
An approval or a rejection, with a reason, on a revision in the queue; pending findings must be acknowledged before approval.
You get
An approval queue, a status on every revision and an audit event for every decision.
It does not
Choose the reviewer, send the letter after approval, or work for a workspace of one: a second account is required to approve.

Checks and intelligence

Fuel surcharge checks

Prototype, early access

Store a carrier's fuel surcharge schedule as a table of diesel-price tiers. For a claim with a ship date and miles, the check reads the weekly U.S. diesel price for that week from the workspace's imported public price series (U.S. EIA data), looks up the table rate and compares it with the fuel line billed. The result is a preview computed on request; it never changes a stored packet.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
The carrier's table (versioned, archived rather than deleted) and each claim's ship date, miles and table.
You get
The expected surcharge, the billed line and the difference, with the price observation and table version used. A cost outlook with ranges, built from bundled public price history (EIA and BLS data via FRED) and checked by walk-forward backtest, is a regression check of the outlook rules, not a promise about prices.
It does not
Fetch anything during a request: price series are imported separately with their provenance. It does not know your contract's fuel clause unless you enter it as a table.

Charge intelligence

Prototype, early access

For each invoice line, the nearest of 175 charge names copied from six public carrier tariffs, with a similarity score. A suggestion appears only above a fixed threshold and only adds an item for review. Two more fixed scores order the work: a documented work-order score over stored amounts, and an explainable similarity between claims from carrier, shared rule ids, amount and perspective, with stated weights. No model is trained on your data; the only memory is your team's own labels.

The measured learning curve, in scoup Labs
What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
Nothing beyond the claim; a label when a suggestion is wrong.
You get
Suggested charge names with scores, a stated order for the claim list, and similar claims with the points behind each match.
It does not
Confirm an amount. The leave-one-carrier-out check on the 175 seed names is a regression check on that set, not an accuracy measure on invoices.

Team charge labels

Prototype, early access

Label a charge description once (detention, linehaul, fuel, accessorial or other) and the label applies to every line in your workspace with the same normalised text. A label that marks a line for review routes it to a person. The label memory is capped per workspace and every change is audited.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
One label per charge description, with its category.
You get
For each line of a claim's accepted documents: the keyword class the rules use, the classifier's suggestion and your team's label, side by side.
It does not
Change an amount, share labels between workspaces, or write the description itself into the audit metadata.

Out and across

CSV and XLSX exports

Prototype, early access

Claims, packets and outcomes stream out as CSV or XLSX batch by batch, so memory stays bounded whatever the size. Text cells are normalised and neutralised against spreadsheet formulas; the XLSX holds inline strings and numbers only, with no formulas, links, macros, drawings or document properties, so a cell can never run anything when opened.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
The same filters as the claim list, and the format.
You get
A file created on request, with an audit event that records its row count and the hash of the exact bytes sent; an interrupted download is recorded as aborted so a truncated file cannot pass as complete.
It does not
Push data into another system; the API is the way to pull it.

API with scoped keys

Prototype, early access

Keys are stored as keyed hashes, each with scopes limited to what its creator may do (read claims, export claims, run imports), a rate limit, an optional expiry and a revocation with a reason. Key management itself requires a signed-in user: a key cannot create, list or revoke keys.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
A name, the scopes and an expiry.
You get
The key once, at creation; a list with each key's status (active, expired or revoked); access to claims, exports and imports within the scopes.
It does not
Act as a user. A key cannot approve a revision, manage users or manage keys.

QuickBooks Online reference data

Prototype, early access

A read-only snapshot of the chart of accounts and the vendor list of a connected QuickBooks Online company, replaced on each sync and stored per workspace. The access token is used for the request and never stored.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
A connection through Intuit's own sign-in, then a sync when you want a fresh snapshot.
You get
Accounts and vendors as reference values in the workspace, with the time of the snapshot.
It does not
Write anything to QuickBooks: no bills, no vendors, no journal entries. It has been tested with recorded responses, not yet against a live Intuit sandbox.

Controls

Hash-linked audit log

Prototype, early access

Every change appends an event to a chain per workspace. Each event's SHA-256 hash covers the previous hash and the event itself, so a row that is altered, removed or reordered breaks the chain, and a verifier reports the first break. The event is written in the same database transaction as the change it records. Metadata never carries invoice text, descriptions or file names.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
Nothing; it is written with every change.
You get
A readable log per claim and per workspace with actor, role, action and time, and a chain verification result.
It does not
Replace an external timestamp or signature. It shows order and integrity inside the database; it is not proof against someone who controls the database itself.

Sign-in protection

Prototype, early access

Passwords are hashed with Argon2id. Owners, administrators and platform staff must use a second factor (an authenticator app, with recovery codes). Sessions use rotating refresh tokens with reuse detection, and repeated failures lock the account for a period. Roles are read from the database on every request, never from a token or header.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
Email and password, and the authenticator code for a privileged role.
You get
Six workspace roles from owner to viewer, invitations limited to roles below your own, and a session that ends when a refresh token is reused.
It does not
Offer single sign-on or passkeys, and it has had no independent assessment.

Workspace isolation

Prototype, early access

Every workspace is a tenant. Tenant tables carry forced row-level security in the database and every request runs in a tenant-scoped transaction, designed to limit cross-tenant reads from any query path, including a bug in the application.

What you enter, what you get, what it does not do
You enter
Nothing; it is the shape of the database.
You get
Claims, documents, labels, keys and the audit chain scoped to your workspace.
It does not
Stand on an independent audit or on production hosting; neither exists yet.
